Playing Star Wars Solo

No GM, no group: one hero, the narrative dice and a journal, with the dice answering your questions about the galaxy.

Why These Dice Suit Solo Play

Solo roleplaying replaces the GM with an oracle: you ask the yes-or-no questions a GM would answer ("Is the hangar guarded?") and a roll decides. Most oracles bolt a "but" onto a plain yes or no. The Star Wars narrative dice already have one. Every roll tells you whether you succeeded and what else happened along the way, so the same dice that run your hero's checks can run the galaxy around them.

Nothing on this page is from the official rulebooks. The oracle, the scene check and the solo Destiny rules are tools for playing alone; anything that changes a rule is labelled house rule.

What you need

  • A character built with the normal rules (see Character Creation), or one of the 75 Sample Characters.
  • A set of narrative dice, or the tool on this page and the Narrative Dice Roller.
  • A d12 (an ordinary numbered one) for the attitude and spark tables, or the tool.
  • A journal: paper, a document or the session log below.

The Oracle

Ask a question that can be answered yes or no. Decide how likely a "yes" is, given what you know about the scene, then roll the oracle pool:

Always roll 2 Ability (green) + 1 Proficiency (yellow) against the opposition for your likelihood. The opposition always includes 1 Challenge (red), plus Difficulty (purple) dice. Together they climb the game's own difficulty ladder:

  • Very likely: Easy, 1 die: 1 Challenge
  • Likely: Average, 2 dice: 1 Challenge + 1 Difficulty
  • Toss-up: Hard, 3 dice: 1 Challenge + 2 Difficulty
  • Unlikely: Daunting, 4 dice: 1 Challenge + 3 Difficulty
  • Very unlikely: Formidable, 5 dice: 1 Challenge + 4 Difficulty

The yellow die is always there so that a Triumph is possible, and the red die so that a Despair is possible. Read the result the way you would read any check:

The roll showsAnswer
Success and net AdvantageYes, and… You get what you asked for, plus something extra.
Success, Advantage and Threat cancelYes.
Success and net ThreatYes, but… It's true, with a cost or complication.
Failure and net AdvantageNo, but… It's not true, but something helps.
Failure, Advantage and Threat cancelNo.
Failure and net ThreatNo, and… It's not true, and things get worse.

Triumph and Despair are twists, on top of the answer. A Triumph means something new and good arrives: an ally, an opening, a clue you didn't ask about. A Despair means something new and bad: an enemy, a breakdown, a betrayal. If you're stuck for what, roll a spark. A roll can show both.

A Triumph comes up 8.3% of the time (1 in 12) and a Despair 8.3% at every likelihood, because there is always exactly one yellow and one red die.

Two things stand out. First, the spread is gentle: even "very unlikely" is a yes more than a quarter of the time, so save it for things that really shouldn't happen. Second, look at where the "buts" fall. When an easy question comes up no, it is usually "no, but", and when a long shot comes up yes, it is usually "yes, but". Good fortune rarely comes clean against the odds, and bad luck rarely comes total when you were favoured. That is the narrative dice doing what they do in a normal game.

Ask only questions where both answers would be interesting. If you already know the answer, or one answer would stop the story, just decide.

When you meet someone whose attitude isn't obvious from the fiction, roll a d12:

Destiny Points for One

The normal rule has each player roll one Force die at the start of a session, so a single player gets a pool of one or two points. That is too thin to matter, and there is no GM to spend the dark side. Two house rules fix both:

House rule: a pool of three. At the start of a session, roll three Force dice. Each light-side pip is a light-side Destiny Point, each dark-side pip a dark-side one. You spend light-side points exactly as the rules say: upgrade one of your checks, upgrade the Difficulty of a check made against you, or introduce a convenient detail. Each spent point flips to the dark side.

House rule: the galaxy spends the dark. At the start of each scene, if the dark-side points outnumber the light-side ones, flip one dark-side point to the light side. The galaxy has spent it: upgrade the Difficulty of your next check in that scene once.

The pool then does what it does at a table. Each time you lean on luck you hand the galaxy a little more power, and it pushes back when the balance tips. Three Force dice hold 4.0 pips on average, so a typical solo session starts with roughly four points.

A party of one

The rules assume a crew of several characters. Alone, a few adjustments help. They are optional house rules:

  • A sidekick (house rule): one companion, such as an astromech, a co-pilot or a loyal muscle, built as a rival. They don't take turns of their own unless you want them to; when they help, give your check a Boost die.
  • Fewer, smaller foes (house rule): use minion groups of two or three rather than five, and one rival rather than a squad. One hero cannot soak a fight built for four.
  • Obligation still bites: if your character has Obligation, Duty or Morality, use it as written. A single character's Obligation rarely triggers, so treat a trigger as the session's main complication.

    Session log

      The log is kept in this browser only.

      Your First Solo Session

      1. Make a hero and a hook. One character, one reason to be somewhere dangerous today: a cargo to deliver, a debt to pay, a message to carry.
      2. Roll the Destiny pool (three Force dice) and, if your hero has Obligation, Duty or Morality, handle it as written.
      3. Frame the first scene. Write one line: where you are and what you expect. Make a scene check.
      4. Play it. When you'd ask a GM something, ask the oracle. When your hero tries something risky, make a normal check with your real dice pool. Let the Advantage, Threat, Triumph and Despair on both kinds of roll shape what happens next.
      5. End the scene when the immediate question is answered. At the next scene, run the galaxy check on the Destiny pool and make a new scene check.
      6. Stop after three or four scenes. Write one line about what's still unresolved. That's next session's hook.

      An Example of Play

      Kessa Vaal is a human smuggler with a light freighter, an astromech sidekick called R7 and a debt to a Hutt-run cartel. Tonight she has to collect a crate from a spaceport on an Outer Rim world without the port authority noticing.

      Destiny. Three Force dice show ○○, ●, ●: 2 light and 2 dark. Pool: 2 light, 2 dark.

      Scene 1: the docking bay. I expect a quiet bay and a nervous contact. Galaxy check: dark doesn't outnumber light, so nothing happens. Scene check: the Force die shows ●, so the scene is as expected.

      Oracle: "Is my contact already here?" Likely, so 2 Ability + 1 Proficiency against 1 Challenge + 1 Difficulty. The green dice show S and A, the yellow S A, the red T and the purple F. That's 2 Success against 1 Failure (1 net Success) and 2 Advantage against 1 Threat (1 net Advantage): Yes, and… He's here, and he has already loaded the crate onto a repulsor sled.

      A real check. A port inspector strolls towards the sled. Kessa tries to bluff her way past: Deception, Cunning 3 and Deception 1, so 1 Proficiency + 2 Ability. The GM's job falls to me: an Average check, 2 Difficulty. I want this badly, so I spend a light Destiny Point to upgrade my pool: 2 Proficiency + 1 Ability. The pool is now 1 light, 3 dark.

      The yellow dice show SS and Triumph, the green a blank; the purple dice show F T and TT. Successes: 2 + 1 (the Triumph) = 3 against 1 Failure, so 2 net Successes. Advantage 0 against 3 Threat, so 3 net Threat. Success, with 3 Threat and a Triumph. The inspector waves the sled through (success), but he logs the freighter's transponder code (Threat). The Triumph is a gift: as he turns away, Kessa sees him pocket a bribe from a cartel enforcer. She now knows who in this port belongs to whom.

      Scene 2: lifting off. I expect a clean take-off. Galaxy check: 3 dark against 1 light, so the galaxy flips one: pool 2 light, 2 dark, and my next check this scene has its Difficulty upgraded once. Scene check: the Force die shows ●●, so the scene is interrupted. Spark: 2 and 11, pursue an old friend. A familiar voice on the comm: Kessa's former co-pilot, now flying for the port authority, ordering her to hold position. Her next Piloting check, to break away, will roll against an upgraded Difficulty.

      That's the loop: ask, roll, interpret, write a line, move on. The interesting decisions are still yours: whether to answer that comm call is a question no oracle can settle.
