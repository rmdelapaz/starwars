Understanding Star Wars RPG Characters

The Star Wars RPG (the narrative dice system originally published by Fantasy Flight Games and carried on today by Edge Studio — you will see either logo depending on the printing) creates characters through a unique combination of Species, Career, and Specializations. Instead of classes and levels, you build and advance characters by spending experience points (XP), which allows for incredible flexibility while maintaining the iconic archetypes that make the galaxy far, far away so compelling.

graph TD A[Species] --> D[Base Characteristics] B[Career] --> E[Career Skills] C[Specialization] --> F[Talent Trees] D --> G[Character Foundation] E --> G F --> G G --> H[Motivation & Obligation] H --> I[Complete Character] I --> J[Force Sensitivity] I --> K[Equipment & Relationships]

The Three Pillars of Character Creation

Every Star Wars character is built on three core elements:

  • Species: Your biological heritage - Human, Twi'lek, Wookiee, etc.
  • Career: Your background and general profession - Smuggler, Diplomat, Bounty Hunter
  • Specialization: Your specific focus within that career - Pilot, Ambassador, Assassin

Real-World Analogy

Think of Star Wars character creation like choosing a profession in our world. Your species is like your cultural background - it influences how you see the world and what advantages you might have. Your career is your general field - like "military" or "entertainment." Your specialization is your specific job within that field - like "fighter pilot" or "musician." Just as a military communications specialist and a military medic have different skills despite the same career background, Star Wars specializations provide focused abilities within broader career paths.

The Role of the Force

Force sensitivity is not a characteristic. In Force and Destiny every career starts with a Force rating of 1; in the other two books a character buys a universal Force-sensitive specialization (Force Sensitive Exile or Force Sensitive Emergent) to gain one. Force-sensitive characters have access to Force powers and can develop along Jedi or Sith paths, but they can also be smugglers, diplomats, or any other career who happen to have a connection to the Force.

Every hero also gets real weapon and armor profiles (a suggested kit bought with the 500 starting credits where the card lists no gear), wound and strain thresholds, soak and defense, and a Notes field that explains each choice and labels the GM calls. Character Creation Process

Creating a Star Wars character involves balancing mechanical effectiveness with compelling narrative hooks that fit the space opera genre.

When to Use These Sample Characters

  • New Players: Jump into the Star Wars universe without overwhelming character creation
  • One-Shots: Quick character selection for convention games or movie marathons
  • Campaign Starters: Pre-made groups with built-in relationships and motivations
  • NPCs: Ready-made supporting characters and antagonists
  • Inspiration: Templates for creating custom characters with similar themes

Era Considerations

These characters are designed for flexibility across different Star Wars eras:

  • Prequel Era: Republic at its height, Jedi Order active, Clone Wars
  • Original Trilogy Era: Imperial oppression, Rebellion, hidden Jedi
  • Sequel Era: New Republic, First Order, emerging Force users
  • High Republic: Jedi at their peak, exploration and discovery

Adversaries: Minion Groups and Rivals

The everyday opposition. Minion groups share one pooled wound threshold (each minion's threshold times the group size), have no strain threshold, and roll their group skills at one rank per minion after the first, so they weaken as the heroes thin them out. A single Critical Injury takes one minion out. Rivals are named individuals with a wound threshold but no strain threshold: strain they would suffer is taken as wounds instead.

Before You Run One: The Missing Numbers

The 75 characters above give you characteristics, skills, talents and gear — everything that is chosen. (The major NPCs, adversaries and mercenaries already list theirs, and the vehicles carry full profiles.) What none of the 75 list are the numbers that get derived, and those are exactly the ones you need the moment someone takes a hit. Work them out once, pencil them onto the entry, and the character is table-ready.

  • Wound Threshold = species base + Brawn. This is how much physical punishment they absorb before falling over.
  • Strain Threshold = species base + Willpower. Strain is fatigue, fear and mental strain — spent voluntarily for extra manoeuvres, and inflicted by social attacks.
  • Soak = Brawn + armour. Subtracted from every hit before it touches the Wound Threshold.
  • Defence = from armour, cover or a ship's shields. Usually 0 for an unarmoured character standing in the open.

Humans use 10 and 10 as their two species bases, which makes them the useful baseline. Other species trade one against the other — tough species buy a higher wound base by accepting a lower strain base — so check the species entry rather than assuming. Taking Kira Nexu from the top of the list: Brawn 2, Willpower 3, human, so Wound Threshold 12, Strain Threshold 13, Soak 2. That took ten seconds and she is now runnable.

Turning a Character Sheet Into an Opponent

Most of these entries are written as player characters, but a GM raiding this list usually wants an adversary. Adversaries come in three tiers, and picking the right one matters more than adjusting any statistic:

TierHow it worksUse it for
MinionOperates as a group sharing one wound threshold. No strain. The group's skill is driven by how many are still standing, so they get worse as you thin them out.Stormtroopers, dock guards, hired muscle — anyone whose job is to lose impressively
RivalAn individual with a wound threshold but no strain threshold (strain it would suffer is taken as wounds instead). Dangerous, and it can suffer Critical Injuries, but going past its wound threshold simply takes it out of the fight.The bounty hunter who keeps turning up, a lieutenant, a recurring nuisance
NemesisBuilt exactly like a player character — both thresholds, critical injuries, the lot. Treat any entry above as a nemesis with no conversion at all.The villain of the arc. Use sparingly; a nemesis should be an event

The practical shortcut: any of the 75 characters on this page is already a nemesis. To demote one to a rival, drop the strain threshold and apply any strain it suffers as wounds. To make minions, keep the two or three skills that define them and discard everything else — nobody at the table will ever ask what a stormtrooper's Willpower is.

Scaling to Your Party

Most of these builds are seasoned characters rather than fresh ones (a brand-new character cannot start with any skill above rank 2), so treat them as having already earned a good deal of XP. Two adjustments come up constantly:

  • If your party is experienced, do not inflate an opponent's characteristics — that makes the maths swingy without making the fight interesting. Add minions, add terrain, or add a second objective competing for the party's attention.
  • If you are short a player, running one of these as an NPC ally works, but hand it to a player rather than yourself. A GM voicing an ally in combat slows every round and quietly steers the party.

NPCs & Villains goes further into making an antagonist memorable rather than merely statted, and Combat & Conflict covers how these thresholds behave under fire.

Campaign Integration

Star Wars campaigns thrive on the intersection of personal stories with galactic events:

  • Obligation as Plot Driver: Use character obligations to create personal stakes in larger conflicts
  • Duty and Purpose: Character duties connect personal goals to faction objectives
  • Force Destiny: Force-sensitive characters can influence major galactic events
  • Relationship Webs: Cross-team relationships create complex alliance and betrayal opportunities

Scaling for Different Play Styles

These characters support various Star Wars campaign styles:

  • Heroic Adventures: Focus on Jedi, Rebels, and righteous smugglers
  • Edge of the Empire: Emphasize criminals, bounty hunters, and survival on the Rim
  • Military Campaigns: Use soldiers, commanders, and ace pilots for war stories
  • Political Intrigue: Diplomats, spies, and corporate characters for courtly drama

Force Usage and Character Development

The Force is central to Star Wars storytelling, even for characters who aren't Force-sensitive. Understanding how Force mechanics integrate with character development enhances gameplay for everyone.

Force-Sensitive Character Growth

Force users develop along multiple paths simultaneously:

  • Power Development: Learning new Force powers and upgrading existing ones
  • Lightsaber Mastery: Advancing through different lightsaber forms and techniques
  • Moral Growth: Navigating the conflict between Light and Dark sides
  • Mentorship: Teaching other characters enhances your own understanding

Non-Force Character Integration

Characters without Force sensitivity remain crucial to Star Wars stories:

  • Technological Solutions: Tech skills that complement Force abilities
  • Social Networks: Contacts and resources Force users cannot access
  • Practical Skills: Piloting, mechanics, and combat expertise
  • Moral Anchors: Providing ethical guidance without mystical complications

Adapting Characters Across Star Wars Eras

The galaxy far, far away spans thousands of years. These characters can be adapted to different time periods with minimal modifications.

Prequel Era (32-19 BBY)

Adapt characters for the height of the Republic and the Clone Wars:

  • Jedi Characters: Full Jedi Order support, temple resources, structured training
  • Military Characters: Clone Wars context, Republic vs Separatist conflicts
  • Political Characters: Senatorial intrigue, Palpatine's rise to power
  • Criminal Characters: War profiteering, black market opportunities

Original Trilogy Era (0-4 ABY)

The classic Rebellion vs Empire timeframe:

  • Rebel Characters: Fighting against overwhelming Imperial oppression
  • Imperial Characters: Maintaining order through strength and fear
  • Independent Characters: Trying to survive between galactic superpowers
  • Force Users: Hidden abilities, secret training, rediscovering lost knowledge

New Republic Era (4-34 ABY)

Rebuilding civilization after the Empire's fall:

  • New Republic Characters: Building democratic institutions
  • Imperial Remnant Characters: Holding onto the old ways
  • Jedi Characters: Rebuilding the Jedi Order from scratch
  • Explorer Characters: Mapping unknown regions, first contact situations

Star Wars Storytelling Techniques

The Hero's Journey in Star Wars

Star Wars follows classic mythological patterns that these characters embody:

  • Call to Adventure: Characters' obligations and duties pull them into larger conflicts
  • Mentor Figures: Older, wiser characters guide younger ones
  • Trials and Tests: Personal growth through facing fears and challenges
  • Return Transformed: Characters emerge changed by their experiences

Destiny and the Force

Even non-Force characters participate in the galaxy's destiny:

  • Destiny Points: Flip them to represent the Force flowing through events
  • Meaningful Coincidences: "Chance" encounters that drive the story forward
  • Moral Choices: Decisions that tip the balance between Light and Dark
  • Legacy Building: Actions that influence future generations

Faction Dynamics

Star Wars thrives on the tension between competing factions:

  • Ideological Conflicts: Freedom vs Order, Republic vs Empire
  • Resource Competition: Control of hyperspace routes, planets, technology
  • Personal Betrayals: Characters switching sides for personal reasons
  • Unlikely Alliances: Former enemies working together against greater threats

Practical Applications

Starting a Campaign

Use these characters to launch different types of Star Wars campaigns:

  • Rebellion Campaigns: Start with Rebel cell characters fighting Imperial oppression
  • Exploration Campaigns: Use explorer teams to discover new worlds and civilizations
  • Criminal Campaigns: Follow smuggling crews and their dangerous cargo runs
  • Force Campaigns: Focus on Jedi training and the struggle between Light and Dark

Convention and One-Shot Games

These pre-generated characters excel in short-form play:

  • Immediate Recognition: Players quickly understand familiar Star Wars archetypes
  • Built-in Motivation: Clear goals and relationships drive action from scene one
  • Balanced Teams: Each character contributes unique capabilities to the group
  • Scalable Complexity: Use simpler characters for new players, complex ones for veterans

Teaching the System

Help new players learn Star Wars RPG mechanics:

  • Career Examples: Show how different careers approach similar challenges
  • Force Demonstration: Include both Force-sensitive and mundane characters
  • Obligation Showcase: Demonstrate how personal complications drive stories
  • Skill Synergy: Show how different characters' skills complement each other

Conclusion

These 75 Star Wars RPG characters, and the villains, hirelings and vehicles that follow them, represent the rich diversity of a galaxy far, far away. From Force-sensitive Jedi to pragmatic smugglers, from Imperial loyalists to Rebel freedom fighters, each character embodies the themes and archetypes that make Star Wars storytelling so compelling.

These pre-generated characters provide the foundation for exploring those themes while delivering the excitement and adventure that defines the Star Wars experience.
