Welcome to the Star Wars RPG

What this game is, which edition this guide covers, and how its narrative dice work.

What Makes Star Wars RPG Special

Imagine you're not just watching Luke Skywalker's journey, but living your own. Star Wars RPG isn't just about rolling dice and adding numbers - it's about becoming part of the most beloved space opera of all time. Think of it like being handed the director's chair of your own Star Wars movie, where every decision you make shapes the galaxy's destiny.

The Orchestra Analogy

If traditional RPGs are like playing solo piano, Star Wars RPG is like conducting a full orchestra. The Game Master is the conductor, players are the musicians, and the dice are the sheet music that guides the improvisation. Together, you create a symphony that would make John Williams proud.

Which Star Wars RPG Is This?

Worth saying plainly, because several very different Star Wars roleplaying games have existed over the years. This guide covers the Star Wars Roleplaying Game by Fantasy Flight Games, now published by Edge Studio: the narrative dice system, built around custom symbol dice instead of numbers. It was published in three parallel core rulebooks, each a complete game on its own:

Core rulebookYou play asIts signature mechanic
Edge of the EmpireSmugglers, bounty hunters, fringers scraping a living on the outer rimObligation — the debts and grudges that follow you
Age of RebellionRebel operatives fighting a war against the EmpireDuty — what you have earned from the Alliance
Force and DestinyForce-sensitives surviving and training in an era that hunts themMorality — your pull between light and dark

The three lines share one engine, so characters from different books sit at the same table without conversion. Most groups own one core book and borrow freely from the others.

A note on publishers

The short history: Fantasy Flight Games took the licence in 2012 and released Edge of the Empire (2013), Age of Rebellion (2014) and Force and Destiny (2015); the line moved to Edge Studio in 2020, so you may see either logo depending on the printing. The rules are the same. If you want this dice engine without the Star Wars setting, the generic version is Genesys (2017).

It is not West End Games' d6 Star Wars game (1987) or Wizards of the Coast's d20 Saga Edition (2007). Those are different games with different rules, so advice written for them (Wild Die, Force Points, Defense scores, d20 rolls) does not apply here.

What You Actually Need to Start

Less than you might think. A core rulebook, pencils, and something to roll:

  • The special dice. The symbols are not printed on ordinary dice, so you need the official set — or a substitute. Many groups never buy physical dice at all.
  • Or a roller. This site has one built in: the Narrative Dice Roller rolls every die type in your browser, free, no set required. It is the cheapest possible way to try the system tonight.
  • Or ordinary dice plus a conversion chart. Workable, but it adds a lookup step to every roll and tends to slow new players down. Use it as a stopgap.
  • Three to five friends. One runs the game, the rest play characters. The system works fine with two players and a GM, and strains above six.

The Heart of the Galaxy - Core Systems

The Narrative Dice System

Instead of adding up numbers, Star Wars RPG uses a narrative dice system. Think of these dice as storytelling companions rather than simple number generators. Each die tells a part of your story.

The Full Set - Seven Dice, Three Jobs

The diagram above shows the four you will meet first, but a complete set has seven dice. They sort into three jobs: dice that help you, dice that oppose you, and one die that answers to nobody.

DieShapeJobWhat it contributes
Boostd6Helps youSmall bonuses — good gear, high ground, a friend's assist
Abilityd8Helps youYour basic capability: one die per point of your characteristic or skill rank, whichever is higher
Proficiencyd12Helps youAn upgraded Ability die (training plus talent). The only die that can roll Triumph
Setbackd6Opposes youMinor hindrances: poor light, an awkward angle
Difficultyd8Opposes youHow hard the task is: Easy 1, Average 2, Hard 3, Daunting 4, Formidable 5
Challenged12Opposes youAn upgraded Difficulty die (skilled opposition, a Destiny Point). The only die that can roll Despair
Forced12NeitherLight- and dark-side pips, rolled for Force powers and for the Destiny Point pool

Notice the symmetry: every helpful die has an opposing twin of the same shape. Boost mirrors Setback, Ability mirrors Difficulty, Proficiency mirrors Challenge. Once you see the pairs, assembling a pool stops feeling like bookkeeping.

The Six Symbols

You are not adding numbers. You are reading symbols, and they cancel each other in pairs before you interpret what is left:

SymbolCancelsMeaning
SuccessFailureDid the thing work? You need at least one net Success; a tie fails.
FailureSuccessIt did not work — but something still happens.
AdvantageThreatA side benefit, independent of whether you succeeded.
ThreatAdvantageA cost or complication, independent of success.
Triumph—Counts as a Success (which Failure can cancel) and grants something dramatic. The Triumph effect itself is never cancelled.
Despair—Counts as a Failure (which Success can cancel) and something goes badly wrong. The Despair effect itself is never cancelled.

The two axes are the whole trick. Success/Failure answers "did it work?" and Advantage/Threat answers "what else happened?" — and they are rolled at the same time, independently. That is why a roll can succeed disastrously or fail usefully, which is exactly how Star Wars actually behaves on screen.

Success vs Failure - It's Not That Simple

Here's where Star Wars RPG becomes brilliant. Success and failure aren't binary states - they're spectrums of possibility. Imagine Han Solo trying to fix the hyperdrive:

Example: Han's Hyperdrive Repair

  • Success + Advantage: "I fix it AND find a way to boost our speed!"
  • Success + Threat: "It's working, but I hear Imperial ships approaching..."
  • Failure + Advantage: "Can't fix it, but I learn something useful for next time"
  • Failure + Threat: "Not only is it broken, but now we're venting atmosphere!"

The Power of "Yes, And..." vs "No, But..."

Traditional RPGs often result in simple yes/no answers. Star Wars RPG transforms every roll into a collaborative storytelling moment. It's like the difference between a light switch and a dimmer - you get infinite variations of brightness rather than just on or off.

Becoming a Hero - Character Creation Philosophy

The Three Pillars of Character Identity

Every memorable Star Wars character rests on three pillars, like a three-legged stool. Remove any one, and the character becomes unstable:

Pillar One

Species & Career

What you are and what you do. This sets your starting skills and your place in the galaxy — the practical foundation everything else is built on.

Pillar Two

Motivation

What you want. The reason your character gets out of the bunk in the morning: a person, a cause, a belief, or a debt owed to themselves.

Pillar Three

Obligation, Duty or Morality

What has a claim on you. Each core rulebook names this differently, but it does the same job: it gives the GM a hook to pull, so your backstory keeps intruding on the plot.

That third pillar is the one newcomers most often skip, and it is the one that makes the game feel like Star Wars. A character with a Motivation is interesting. A character with a Motivation and something dragging at them is a story. Luke wants to leave Tatooine; the claim on him is his family and, later, his father. Take away either half and the arc collapses.

Species + Career = Your Starting Point

Think of this combination as your character's "origin story trailer." A Twi'lek Smuggler has a completely different flavor than a Human Smuggler or a Twi'lek Diplomat. It's like choosing the genre and setting of your personal Star Wars movie.

The Force - More Than Magic

Understanding Force Rating

The Force isn't a magic system - it's a relationship. Your Force Rating represents how deeply you're connected to the living energy that binds the galaxy together. Mechanically, it is the number of Force dice you roll when you use a power; Force and Destiny careers start at 1, and Force Rating talents raise it. Think of it like learning to play music: Force Rating 1 is knowing basic chords, while higher ratings are like becoming a virtuoso.

Light Side vs Dark Side - The Eternal Choice

Every Force power can be fueled by light or dark side energy. It's like choosing between solar power and burning fossil fuels - one is sustainable and harmonious, the other is quick and destructive. The game reinforces this mechanically: a light-side character who uses dark pips must flip a light Destiny Point to dark and suffer 1 strain per dark pip, and in Force and Destiny gains Conflict that drags on their Morality.

When Does Your Story Happen?

The rulebooks lean toward the classic trilogy years, but the engine is not bolted to them. Picking an era is really picking a power balance — how much authority is watching, and how alone your characters are:

EraWhat it feels like at the table
The High RepublicJedi are numerous, respected and openly deployed. The only era where playing a Jedi means playing an institution rather than a fugitive. Frontier exploration, not insurgency.
The Clone WarsOpen galactic warfare with clear front lines. Suits military campaigns and morally compromised missions where both sides have a point.
Reign of the EmpireThe Andor register: no heroic Rebellion yet, just surveillance, bureaucracy and small acts that cost enormously. Excellent for tense, low-fire-fight play.
Age of RebellionThe default. A functioning Alliance, a clear enemy, and room for both heists and dogfights.
The New RepublicThe Mandalorian and Ahsoka window. Central authority is thin, warlords and bounty guilds fill the gaps — the natural home for Edge of the Empire play.
Off the map entirelySkeleton Crew showed the galaxy works fine at kid-adventure scale, and The Acolyte went back a century before the films. You are not obliged to sit near anything from the movies.

A practical suggestion for a first campaign: pick the era whose pressure you want, not the one with your favourite characters in it. Players usually enjoy being the most interesting people in the room, which is hard if Vader is standing in it. Campaign Creation works through era selection properly, and Galactic Politics covers the factions each era puts in play.

Bringing It All Together - Sample Scenes

Scene: Cantina Confrontation

Setup: Your character needs information from a nervous Rodian in the cantina, but Imperial stormtroopers just walked in.

Different Approaches, Different Dice:

  • Diplomat: Charm + Presence vs. Easy difficulty (1 purple die)
  • Smuggler: Deception + Cunning vs. Average difficulty (2 purple dice)
  • Force User: Coercion + Willpower vs. Hard difficulty (3 purple dice), with Force dice added through an upgrade of the Influence power

Potential Outcomes:

Success + Advantage: You get the info AND the Rodian offers to help you escape
Success + Threat: You get the info BUT the stormtroopers notice the conversation  
Failure + Advantage: No info BUT you overhear something useful from another patron
Failure + Threat: No info AND the Imperials start asking questions about YOU

Try It at the Table

Activity One: Dice Pool Assembly

A pool is built by comparing skill rank against characteristic. Take the larger of the two as your number of dice, then upgrade a number of them equal to the smaller value from Ability to Proficiency. Try these:

  1. A skilled pilot (Piloting 3, Agility 3) attempting a difficult atmospheric entry
  2. A novice mechanic (Mechanics 1, Intellect 2) trying to repair a damaged blaster
  3. A Force-sensitive character using Move to lift a heavy cargo container
Check your answers
  1. Three Proficiency dice, against the difficulty the GM sets. Skill 3 and characteristic 3 means three dice, all three upgraded — a specialist operating at the top of their game.
  2. One Proficiency and one Ability. Characteristic 2 is the larger value, so two dice; skill 1 upgrades one of them. Notice how a low skill with a decent characteristic still gets you rolling.
  3. Force dice equal to their Force Rating, as a Force power check. There is no difficulty to beat: they spend light-side pips to make the power work, and something as big as a cargo container needs the right upgrades to the Move power.

Activity Two: Result Interpretation

Remember to cancel opposing symbols first, then read what survives. Describe what happens:

  • 2 Success, 1 Advantage, 1 Threat
  • 1 Failure, 2 Advantage
  • 3 Success, 1 Triumph, 2 Threat
Check your answers
  • Two net Success, nothing else. The Advantage and Threat cancel exactly, so the task simply works with no side effects — a clean, unremarkable win.
  • Failure with two Advantage. The task does not work, but two good things come out of the attempt: you spot the real problem, or you keep hold of the tool you were about to drop. This combination is the heart of the system — narrate the failure first, then the compensation.
  • Four Success (the Triumph counts as one), a Triumph, and two Threat. A decisive win with a bill attached. The Triumph effect is never cancelled, so spend it on something structural — the door stays open behind you — while the two Threat cost you time, ammunition, or the attention of someone you would rather not have attracted.

Activity Three: Character Concept Creation

Create three different character concepts using different species/career combinations. For each, write:

  • A one-sentence origin story
  • Their primary motivation
  • One obligation or duty that drives their adventures

Why This Matters Beyond Gaming

Star Wars RPG teaches valuable real-world skills:

  • Collaborative Problem Solving: Like team projects in the workplace
  • Improvisation: Thinking on your feet when plans change
  • Narrative Thinking: Understanding cause and effect in complex systems
  • Empathy: Seeing situations from different perspectives
  • Creative Communication: Expressing ideas in engaging ways
