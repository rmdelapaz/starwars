Campaign Foundations

The core ideas, table agreements and structure that a Star Wars campaign is built on.

Where a Campaign Starts

Building a Star Wars RPG campaign is like constructing a lightsaber - it requires patience, understanding of the Force (your story), and the right crystals (elements) to make it shine. Just as a Jedi must understand both the light and dark sides of the Force, a Game Master must balance hope and conflict, heroism and moral complexity.

Understanding the Force of Storytelling

The Force in Star Wars isn't just magic - it's the binding narrative thread that connects all living things. In your campaign, think of the Force as your central theme or the emotional core that drives everything forward.

The Three Pillars of Campaign Force

🌟 Hope vs Despair

Every Star Wars story balances light against darkness. Your campaign should have moments of triumph and setbacks, just like Luke's journey from farm boy to Jedi Knight.

⚔️ Conflict & Choice

Meaningful decisions define heroes. Present your players with choices that matter - save the hostages or pursue the villain? Trust the suspicious ally or go it alone?

🤝 Connection & Legacy

Star Wars is about relationships - mentors and apprentices, friends and rivals. Build connections between characters and show how their actions ripple through the galaxy.

Constructing Your Galaxy

Think of the Star Wars galaxy like a vast ocean with countless islands (star systems). Each island has its own culture, dangers, and opportunities. You don't need to map every system - just the ones that matter to your story.

The Galactic Timeline Approach

Old Republic Era

When: Thousands of years before A New Hope
Feel: Ancient mysteries, powerful Force users, grand civilizations
Perfect for: Epic quests, exploring Jedi/Sith history, lost technologies

The High Republic

When: Roughly 500-100 years before A New Hope
Feel: Jedi at their confident height, frontier expansion, optimism with cracks in it
Perfect for: Exploration, disaster response, and the only era where playing a Jedi means playing an institution rather than a fugitive

Clone Wars Era

When: 22-19 years before A New Hope
Feel: Galactic war, political intrigue, fall of heroes
Perfect for: Military campaigns, moral complexity, watching democracy crumble

Reign of the Empire

When: 19-5 years before A New Hope
Feel: Surveillance, paperwork and fear — no organised Rebellion to join yet
Perfect for: Tense, low-firefight play where small acts of defiance cost enormously. The Andor register

Imperial Era

When: 5 years before to 4 years after A New Hope
Feel: Oppression, rebellion, hope against impossible odds
Perfect for: Underdog stories, smuggling, fighting tyranny

New Republic Era

When: 4-34 years after A New Hope
Feel: Rebuilding, thin central authority, warlords and bounty guilds filling the gaps
Perfect for: Frontier work and salvage — the Mandalorian and Ahsoka window, and the natural home for Edge of the Empire play

Two practical notes. First, pick the era whose pressure you want rather than the one containing your favourite characters — players enjoy being the most interesting people in the room, which is difficult if Vader is standing in it. Second, you are not obliged to sit near the films at all: The Acolyte went back a century before them and Skeleton Crew showed the galaxy works fine at kid-adventure scale.

Crafting Compelling Characters

In Star Wars, characters aren't just stats and abilities - they're people with dreams, fears, and destinies. Think of character creation like building a droid: you need the right parts (background, motivation, flaw) to make it come alive.

The Hero's Journey in Character Development

Every great Star Wars character follows a transformation arc. Luke starts as a whiny farm boy and becomes a confident Jedi. Anakin begins as a hopeful child and becomes Darth Vader. Your players' characters should have similar potential for growth and change.

Designing Meaningful Conflict

Conflict in Star Wars operates on multiple levels, like the layers of a planet's atmosphere. Surface conflicts might be about escaping Imperial troops, but deeper conflicts explore themes of redemption, sacrifice, and the nature of good and evil.

Real-World Parallels

Star Wars draws from real history and current events. The Empire reflects fascist regimes, the Rebellion mirrors resistance movements, and the Jedi's fall echoes the collapse of democratic institutions. Use these parallels to add depth and relevance to your campaign.

Practical Applications

Session Zero: Your Campaign's Foundation

Before you even roll dice, hold a "Session Zero" - think of it as the Jedi Council meeting where you agree the rules and expectations for your campaign.

Essential Questions to Discuss:

  • What era interests everyone? (This determines the galaxy's political state)
  • What type of heroes do you want to be? (Smugglers and bounty hunters suit Edge of the Empire, Rebel operatives Age of Rebellion, Force users Force and Destiny; the three core books share one dice system and mix freely)
  • How serious vs. lighthearted? (Rogue One vs. A New Hope tone)
  • What themes matter to your group? (Redemption, friendship, justice, etc.)

The Unglamorous Half: Logistics

Campaigns die of scheduling far more often than they die of bad storytelling. Settle these while everyone is still enthusiastic, because enthusiasm is a poor substitute for a calendar:

  • How often, and for how long? Fortnightly three-hour sessions that actually happen beat weekly five-hour ones that keep collapsing.
  • What counts as quorum? Decide now whether you play with someone missing. Groups that never decide this end up cancelling by default.
  • How does a session end? Stopping on time, mid-arc, is a skill. Ending on a cliffhanger beats ending because everyone is exhausted.
  • Who brings what? Dice, snacks, and the character sheets someone will inevitably forget.

Safety Tools: Agreeing the Edges

Star Wars runs on torture droids, amputation, slavery, genocide and family betrayal. That range is exactly why the setting has lasted — but it also means a table can wander somewhere a player did not sign up for, usually by accident and usually mid-scene. Safety tools are how you find those edges before you hit them. They take ten minutes, and they are standard practice now rather than an optional courtesy.

  • Lines — content that will not appear at all. Collect them privately if anyone prefers; a written list passed round works better than going around the table aloud.
  • Veils — content that exists but happens off screen. The interrogation occurs; you cut away and rejoin afterwards. Most Star Wars darkness plays better veiled anyway.
  • An escape hatch — one agreed signal, a card or a word, that any player including the GM can use to rewind or skip a scene. No explanation required, no discussion afterwards. The point is that it costs nothing to use.
  • A check-in after session one. People rarely know their own edges in the abstract. Ask again once everyone has seen what the game is actually like.

Two things make these work. First, they apply to you as well — running a scene that is upsetting you is not professionalism. Second, using one is never up for debate: a veil invoked mid-scene is honoured immediately and play moves on. A table that argues about a safety tool has just demonstrated why it needed one.

What the Game Master Owes the Table

Session zero usually covers what the players want. Stating the reverse prevents most GM burnout:

  • Preparation proportional to the group's investment — and no more. A GM who prepares four hours for a group that forgets the plot weekly will quit by month three.
  • Consistency. Rulings can be wrong; they should not be arbitrary. If you rule something on the fly, write it down and keep it.
  • Spotlight discipline. Everyone gets a scene that is theirs. That is a scheduling problem, not a talent problem, and it belongs to the GM.
  • The right to say "let me think about it." You may rule provisionally and correct it next week.

The Three-Act Structure for Campaigns

Structure your campaign like a Star Wars movie trilogy:

Act I: The Spark

Characters meet, discover the threat, and commit to action. Like Luke finding Leia's message and joining the Rebellion.

Act II: The Struggle

Complications arise, victories are costly, and characters face their greatest challenges. Like the Empire striking back and Han being frozen.

Act III: The Resolution

Final confrontation, character growth pays off, and the main conflict resolves. Like Luke redeeming Vader and the Empire's fall.

Try It at the Table

Exercise 1: Galaxy Mapping

Create a simple star map with 5-7 systems. For each system, write one sentence describing:

  • What makes it unique
  • What conflict exists there
  • How it connects to the larger story

Exercise 2: Character Motivations

Write a character with the following framework:

  • Dream: What they want most
  • Fear: What they're afraid of losing
  • Lie: What false belief holds them back
  • Truth: What they need to learn

Exercise 3: Conflict Layers

Take a simple scenario (like "rescue the prisoners") and add three layers:

  • Surface: The immediate problem
  • Hidden: The deeper issue
  • Personal: How it affects each character
A worked example for Exercise 3

Scenario: rescue the prisoners from an Imperial holding facility.

  • Surface: Twelve prisoners, one transport, a garrison that calls for reinforcements in six minutes. This layer is a logistics puzzle and it is what the players will engage with first.
  • Hidden: The prisoners were arrested on information sold by a broker the party has used before and will want to use again. Rescuing them exposes the leak. This layer turns a raid into a decision.
  • Personal: One prisoner recognises a character — same homeworld, same failed evacuation years ago — and remembers them leaving. This layer costs you nothing to prepare and is the part they will still be discussing months later.

Notice the pattern: the surface layer is a problem to solve, the hidden layer is a price to weigh, and the personal layer is a wound to press. You rarely need all three in one session, but a scenario with only the surface layer is the one players forget on the drive home.

Further Exploration

Ready to dive deeper into the galaxy? Consider these advanced topics:

  • Force Traditions: Explore non-Jedi Force users like Nightsisters, Guardians of the Whills, or ancient Sith cults
  • Political Intrigue: Study how the Senate worked, corporate interests, and planetary governments
  • Technology Integration: How do droids, hyperspace, and the Force interact in your stories?
  • Moral Complexity: When is the dark side justified? How do good people make bad choices?
